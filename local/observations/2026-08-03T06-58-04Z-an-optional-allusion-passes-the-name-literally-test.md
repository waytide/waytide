# An allusion the reader need not catch passes the name-literally test, and the rule does not say so

**Status:** a working hypothesis under discovery, surfaced by naming
*"Waytide: Human-Agent Relations"*. It records a case the name-literally-not-by-analogy rule
does not distinguish and its own test already admits. It proposes no change. It is promoted once
it is decided whether the rule should state the distinction, and whether an optional allusion is
a thing to permit or merely a thing not forbidden.

**The case.** *Human-Agent Relations* alludes to C-3PO's self-introduction, *"I am C-3PO,
human-cyborg relations."* For a technical reader the allusion is most of its appeal. It is also
an accurate, literal description of what Waytide governs — the relationship between a human and
an agent — to a reader who has never seen the film.

**The rule's own test already admits it.** name-literally-not-by-analogy asks: *"can the reader
understand it without being taught a mapping? If they must memorize 'X stands for Y,' it
fails."* Nothing has to be memorized here. *Relations* means relations; *human* and *agent* are
the two parties. The name passes on its face.

**What the rule does not distinguish is required from optional.** Its rejected example is *"the
menu problem"*, where the reader "must learn and carry the mapping (menu = the AI's option list,
dish = option, cooking = originating)". That name is **unintelligible until decoded** — the
mapping is not a bonus, it is the whole content. An optional allusion is the opposite shape:

| | without the reference | with it |
|---|---|---|
| **"the menu problem"** | meaningless | means something |
| **"Human-Agent Relations"** | means the right thing | means the right thing, and lands |

**So the distinguishing property is not whether a name alludes to something — it is whether the
literal reading stands alone.** Where it does, the allusion is a **second layer**: it rewards
the reader who catches it and costs nothing to the reader who does not. The rule's stated
reason — that "every analogy, code, or academic term is a toll the reader pays on each
encounter" — does not reach it, because there is no toll. A reader who misses the reference
pays nothing and loses nothing.

**A second thing the allusion did here, which may not generalize.** C-3PO is a protocol droid
who **never decides** — he advises, calculates odds, and objects, and is overruled. That is the
agent-proposes-the-human-decides stance carried inside the reference. So the allusion did not
merely decorate an accurate name; it supplied a nuance the literal words omit. Whether that is a
property of good allusions or a happy accident of this one is not established by a single case.

**The reason to be careful.** The rule exists because vividness is persuasive and precision is
not, and "the literal reading stands alone" is a test the person choosing the name applies to
their own name — which is exactly the position in which people are worst at judging. A stated
permission would be used to license names whose literal reading is thin and whose real content
is the reference. The safer form of this may be no permission at all, leaving an optional
allusion as something the existing test already lets through rather than something the rule
invites.

**What is not settled:** whether the rule should state the distinction; whether it should be
framed as a permission or only as a clarification of the existing test; and whether the
carries-a-nuance property generalizes beyond this one name.
